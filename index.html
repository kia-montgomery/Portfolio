What This Demonstrates

The Scope Statement is where scope creep starts — or gets stopped. It's not the charter (that's authorization) and not the WBS (that's decomposition). The Scope Statement is the plain-language agreement on exactly what will be delivered, what will not be delivered, and what criteria define success. At IBM, this is the document the PM references every single time a stakeholder asks for something new.

The explicit exclusions section is the most important part. What you say you will NOT do is often more powerful than what you say you will do — because it prevents conversations about adding scope later.

What This Demonstrates

Feature prioritization prevents the team from building the wrong things in the right order. MoSCoW alone is too binary — everything becomes a Must. Weighted scoring adds rigor: each feature is scored on business value, effort, risk reduction, and strategic alignment, producing a data-driven ranking that the team and stakeholders can defend. At IBM, this matrix governs what goes into each PI and what gets deferred.

Scoring Methodology

Scoring Dimensions
  • Business Value (1–5): Direct impact on KPIs and charter objectives
  • Effort (1–5 inverse): Lower effort = higher score
  • Risk Reduction (1–5): Does this reduce a High/Critical risk?
  • Strategic Alignment (1–5): Alignment with IBM engagement goals
Weighted Formula
  • Business Value × 0.40
  • Effort (inverse) × 0.25
  • Risk Reduction × 0.20
  • Strategic Alignment × 0.15
  • Max score: 5.0
MoSCoW Rules
  • Must: Score ≥4.0 OR charter KPI dependency
  • Should: Score 3.0–3.9 OR exec-requested
  • Could: Score 2.0–2.9
  • Won't: Score <2.0 OR out of scope

The Definition of Done is the team's quality contract with itself. This DoD operates at three levels: what must be true for a story to be accepted, what must be true for a sprint to be complete, and what must be true before a release goes to production.

Acceptance criteria met — all criteria from the sprint planning agreement verified by QA or BA
Unit tests written and passing — minimum 80% code coverage for new features
Peer code review completed — at least one other developer has reviewed and approved
Regression tests run — no new failures introduced in existing functionality
Story demoed to Product Owner — PO has accepted the story in the sprint review or async
Jira ticket updated — status set to Done; test evidence linked; AC marked complete
No P1 or P2 bugs open — all critical/high defects resolved before story is closed
Documentation updated — inline comments, API docs, or user guide updated if applicable

All committed stories at Story DoD — no stories partially done unless formally deferred with Scrum Master approval
Sprint goal evaluated — met / partially met / not met decision documented with rationale

PMO Setup Guide:
Day 1 to Steady State

IBM Consulting · Agile PMO · Setup Playbook for Senior Consultants

Timeline

Framework

What This Demonstrates

A PMO doesn't build itself. This guide captures what a PM must do to establish the operating infrastructure of a project — governance, tooling, reporting cadence, templates, and stakeholder rhythm — before any real delivery work can begin. At IBM, failing to stand up the PMO properly in the first two weeks is one of the most common causes of chaotic projects. This is how you avoid it.

1
Day 1: Orient Yourself
Before you touch a single deliverable — read the SOW, find the org chart, identify who has actual decision authority (it's rarely the person with the fanciest title), and schedule your first stakeholder calls. Your first week is about listening, not building. Ask: What's already in flight? What's broken? What does success look like to the client — and is it realistic?
2
Days 2–5: Stand Up the Core Infrastructure
Create the project folder structure in SharePoint (by phase: Initiation → Planning → Execution → Close). Set up Jira: create the project, configure epics, define issue types, and build the initial backlog. Create the Teams/Slack channel. Establish naming conventions that everyone will follow. These feel like small things — they prevent months of confusion.
3
Week 1: Draft Foundational Documents
Project Charter (get it signed before Week 2 ends). RACI Matrix. Contact Directory. Communication Plan. Change Request Log template. Risk Register (first pass — 5–10 risks minimum). These are the documents every stakeholder will refer to when things go sideways. Draft them now, before chaos forces improvisation.
4
Week 1: Run the Project Kickoff
Facilitate the kickoff meeting with all key stakeholders — not a slideshow, a working session. Get the charter signed, roles confirmed, SLAs agreed, and the first set of action items logged. Distribute minutes within 24 hours. The kickoff sets the tone for every meeting that follows — run it with structure and discipline.
5
Week 2: Establish the Reporting Cadence
Schedule every recurring meeting for the life of the project — daily standups, bi-weekly sprint reviews, monthly steering committees, and executive briefings. Build your status report template. Decide: what is the one number each executive needs to see every week? Build your KPI tracking from Day 1, not after the project is in trouble.
6
Week 2: Build the Schedule and Budget Baseline
Create the Gantt chart — phased, with owners, dates, and durations. Get it approved and baselined. Build the budget tracker with category-level breakdown. Set up the labor utilization report. At IBM, your financial reporting ties directly to SAP Concur and the SOW — get familiar with those systems immediately.
7
Month 1: Move Into Steady State
By the end of Month 1, the PMO infrastructure should be invisible — it just works. Every stakeholder knows when they'll hear from you, what format the reports come in, and who to call when something goes wrong. Your job shifts from setup to monitoring: tracking actuals vs. plan, catching risks before they become issues, and keeping the team focused on the sprint goal.
